# Diagnostic Criteria

For most PRRT2-related conditions, the diagnosis is built from **clinical features**, **family history**, and **genetic confirmation**, taken together. This page covers how clinicians formally recognize the most common presentations — especially PKD — and the conditions PRRT2 events are most often mistaken for.

## How a PRRT2 diagnosis is built

A PRRT2-related diagnosis usually emerges from three sources of information, considered together:

1. **The clinical picture** — what the episodes look like, how long they last, what triggers them, and what the person is like between episodes.
2. **The family history** — whether relatives have had similar episodes, infantile seizures, migraines, or other unexplained neurological events.
3. **Genetic testing** — confirmation of a pathogenic PRRT2 variant, as described in [How to Get Tested](how-to-get-tested.md).

No single one of these is enough on its own. A clinical picture consistent with PKD makes PRRT2 likely; family history strengthens the case; a genetic result either confirms it or redirects the search.

## Established clinical criteria for PKD

PKD has the clearest formal diagnostic criteria of the PRRT2-related conditions. The widely used framework — developed by Bruno and colleagues — looks for the following features:

* an **identified kinesigenic trigger** — episodes reliably brought on by sudden voluntary movement, or sometimes startle,
* **short duration** of episodes, typically under one minute,
* **no loss of consciousness or pain** during episodes,
* **normal neurological examination** between episodes, with other causes (such as structural brain disease) excluded,
* **effective control with carbamazepine** or related medications, where treatment is tried,
* **onset between roughly age 1 and 20 years** in classical presentations.

Family history of similar episodes — or of related PRRT2 phenotypes such as BFIS — supports the diagnosis but is not required. Sporadic (non-familial) PKD also occurs.

## Criteria for the other PRRT2 conditions

Formal criteria for the other PRRT2-related conditions are less standardized, but the logic is the same — a recognizable clinical pattern, supported by family history where present, confirmed by genetic testing.

* **BFIS / self-limited familial infantile epilepsy** is diagnosed in infants with focal seizures beginning typically within the first year, normal development, and (usually) resolution by early childhood. Family history strongly supports the diagnosis.
* **Hemiplegic migraine** is diagnosed by the international migraine criteria — fully reversible motor weakness as part of the aura, alongside the other features of migraine with aura. PRRT2 is one of several genes associated with familial forms.
* **PED** is diagnosed by an exertional (rather than kinesigenic) trigger, in the appropriate clinical context.

## Differential diagnosis — what PRRT2 events are often mistaken for

Because the episodes are brief, dramatic, and paroxysmal, PRRT2-related events are frequently misidentified. The most common confusions:

* **Epileptic seizures** — especially because consciousness can _look_ altered to an observer, even when it is fully preserved.
* **Functional or psychogenic movement disorders** — sometimes assigned when an episode is brief and unfamiliar.
* **Tic disorders** — particularly when episodes are brief and stereotyped.
* **Panic or anxiety** — particularly with the premonitory sensation that often precedes PKD.

Distinguishing these from PRRT2-related events requires a clinician familiar with paroxysmal movement disorders. As covered in [How to Get Tested](how-to-get-tested.md), this kind of expertise is most often found at **academic movement-disorder programs** and **specialty clinics** — not always at the first stop in a local healthcare system.

{% hint style="warning" %}
**🧭 Foundation Note**

_The PRRT2 Foundation’s own synthesis and lived experience, offered alongside the established science — not as established medical fact._

The formal diagnostic criteria for PKD were developed with **classical, pediatric, familial presentations** in mind. They are excellent for those cases. They are less reliable when the picture is atypical — when symptoms begin in adulthood, when the family history looks silent, when episodes resist the brief, kinesigenic stereotype.

PRRT2.org's view is that a person whose history "almost" fits but does not satisfy every criterion is still worth investigating with genetic testing. Criteria are tools for organized thinking; they are not gatekeepers. **The genetic test is what tells you whether the gene is there** — and a positive result reframes a clinical picture that the criteria alone might have set aside.
{% endhint %}
