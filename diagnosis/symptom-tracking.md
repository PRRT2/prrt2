# Symptom Tracking

Careful self-tracking is one of the most useful — and most underrated — tools both for getting a PRRT2 diagnosis right and for managing it over time. Episodes are brief, often happen between appointments, and frequently look very different to the person experiencing them than to an observer. Your record is often the most reliable data your clinician has.

This page covers what to track, how to do it without making it a burden, and how to use it in appointments.

## Why tracking matters

A specialist evaluating you for a paroxysmal condition needs to understand:

* **The pattern** — when episodes happen, how often, and whether the rate is stable, increasing, or decreasing.
* **The triggers** — what reliably brings them on (sudden movement, exertion, stress, sleep, hormones, illness, fatigue, hunger).
* **The character** — what the body does during the episode, and what the person experiences.
* **The response to treatment** — when medications change, whether episodes change in step.

Without a record, even an attentive patient struggles to answer these questions accurately. A good log answers them directly.

## What to track for each episode

For each episode, even a brief note covering the following is enormously useful:

* **Date and time.**
* **Trigger** — what happened in the seconds or minutes before? Standing up suddenly? Walking? Startle? Stress? Hunger? Hormonal phase? Or no clear trigger?
* **Warning sensation** — many people with PKD describe a brief sensation (tightening, tingling, a "wave") in the moments before. Did you have one? Where?
* **Duration** — roughly how long, from start to feeling normal again. Seconds? A minute? Longer?
* **Body parts affected** — which limb, both sides, face, voice.
* **Character** — were the movements stiff and twisting (dystonic), jerky (choreic), repetitive (tic-like), or something else? Were you aware throughout?
* **Recovery** — did you feel fully normal afterward, or was there fatigue, soreness, or anything lingering?
* **Context** — what were you doing? Where? Were others present?

For longer-term patterns also note:

* **Frequency over time** — episodes per week or per day.
* **Medication changes** — start date, dose, response over the following weeks.

## How to do this without making it a chore

Tracking does not need to be elaborate. A few options that work well:

* **A simple notebook** — date, time, one or two lines per episode.
* **A note-taking app on your phone** — fast to use the moment an episode passes.
* **A spreadsheet** — easy for spotting patterns over months.
* **Calendar entries** — useful for showing density at a glance.
* **A symptom-tracking app** designed for movement disorders or chronic conditions. Several exist; ask your specialist whether they have a recommendation.

The best system is the one you will actually use. Start with whatever fits your life and refine as you go.

## Video, when possible

If you can safely capture a brief episode on video — with consent of anyone present, and only where it is safe to do so — a short clip is often more informative to a specialist than any verbal description. Movement-disorder specialists are skilled at reading video.

If you record video, also note the time and any trigger. The combination is far more useful than either alone.

## Bringing your record to appointments

A clear, organized record makes a specialist visit more productive. A few practical points:

* **Prepare a summary before the visit** — frequency, recent changes, treatment response, any new pattern.
* **Bring representative video**, if you have it.
* **Identify what is hardest, and what you most want to address.** Specialist visits are time-limited; knowing your priorities helps you use them well.
* **Ask for your record to be added to your chart** so future clinicians can see what was tracked.

**🧭 PRRT2.org Perspective**

_PRRT2.org's own perspective — our synthesis and lived experience, offered alongside the established science, not as established medical fact._

In our community, self-tracking is consistently one of the things that **changes how a clinician thinks** about a patient. A neatly organized record of episodes, triggers, and responses to medication takes a presentation that might otherwise look vague or atypical and turns it into a recognizable pattern.

PRRT2.org's view is that the patient is the only person present for every single episode — which makes the patient the best primary source for the data that matters. A good record is not just helpful; for paroxysmal conditions, it is often the single most useful thing a person brings into the clinic.
