# Understanding Your Results

A genetic test returns one of several possible answers. This page explains what each kind of result actually means — and what to do next.

## The main possible outcomes

A PRRT2 (or broader panel) result usually falls into one of these categories:

* **Pathogenic variant** — a change in PRRT2 known to cause disease. This **confirms a PRRT2 diagnosis**.
* **Likely pathogenic** — strong evidence the variant causes disease, but not yet at the highest level of certainty. Usually treated similarly to pathogenic in clinical practice.
* **Variant of uncertain significance (VUS)** — the test found a change in the gene, but current evidence is insufficient to classify it as disease-causing or benign. VUS results often need a closer look.
* **Likely benign** or **benign** — the variant identified is not believed to cause disease.
* **No variant identified** — the test did not find a relevant change.

These categories come from a standardized framework developed by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG). They are interpretations of **evidence**, not fixed truths — and a variant's classification can change over time as more cases are studied.

## What "pathogenic" means in practice

A pathogenic PRRT2 variant **confirms the genetic diagnosis**. In most people this also explains the symptoms, though the connection between gene and lived experience is not strictly one-to-one — see [Inheritance & Genetics](../overview/inheritance-and-genetics.md) for the reasons (incomplete penetrance, variable expressivity).

A pathogenic result usually:

* allows treatment decisions informed by the gene,
* makes **cascade testing** of relatives straightforward — testing the specific known variant in a parent, sibling, or child is inexpensive and direct,
* and brings closure to a diagnostic journey that, for many people, has been long.

The most common variant is **c.649dupC**, found in families worldwide. Other pathogenic variants exist, including deletions affecting the gene.

## What a VUS means — and doesn't

A variant of uncertain significance is one of the most confusing results to receive. It is a statement about **the evidence** — not a statement that the variant is either harmful or harmless. With time and additional research, VUS findings are often reclassified: sometimes to pathogenic, sometimes to benign.

If you receive a VUS in PRRT2:

* **Do not panic, and do not dismiss it.** It needs interpretation in context.
* A **genetic counselor or clinical geneticist** can review the variant in resources like ClinVar and assess the strength of evidence.
* **Family testing** — looking at whether the variant tracks with symptoms in other relatives — is often the most informative next step.
* Variant classifications are reviewed periodically. Ask whether your lab will **re-contact** you if the classification changes.

## What "no variant found" means — and doesn't

A negative result usually means the test did not find a known pathogenic change in PRRT2. It does **not** always mean PRRT2 is ruled out:

* Some tests cover only the coding portion of the gene. Variants outside that region — in regulatory or intronic areas — may not be captured.
* Certain kinds of changes (large deletions or duplications) may require a different testing method.
* If clinical suspicion remains high, a more comprehensive test (deletion/duplication analysis, or whole-exome sequencing) may be appropriate.

If PRRT2 truly is negative but symptoms persist, other genes may be worth exploring — for example **SLC2A1** for paroxysmal exertional dyskinesia, or sodium channel genes for related epilepsies.

## Heterozygous vs homozygous

Most PRRT2-related conditions involve a single altered copy of the gene — a **heterozygous** result. This is the standard pattern in autosomal dominant inheritance.

In rare cases, both copies of the gene are altered — a **homozygous** or **compound heterozygous** result. This is uncommon, and it tends to produce more severe presentations, sometimes including features such as intellectual disability or movement disorders extending beyond the typical PRRT2 picture. Homozygous results warrant close specialist follow-up.

## Working with a genetic counselor

A **genetic counselor** is specifically trained to help patients and families understand results, plan family testing, and discuss reproductive options. For any complex or ambiguous result — especially a VUS, a homozygous finding, or an unexpected outcome — meeting with a counselor is one of the most useful steps you can take.

Certified genetic counselors can be found through the National Society of Genetic Counselors at [findageneticcounselor.com](https://findageneticcounselor.com).

**🧭 Foundation Note**

_The PRRT2 Foundation’s own synthesis and lived experience, offered alongside the established science — not as established medical fact._

A confirmed PRRT2 result is rarely just about the person tested. In our experience, it is the moment a family's scattered medical history — the cousin with infantile seizures, the aunt with migraines, the parent with brief, unexplained movement episodes — begins to come into focus.

The single most useful thing many people do after a positive result is to **share it with relatives** and encourage cascade testing where appropriate. A pathogenic variant identified in one person costs the family almost nothing extra to look for in others. And for relatives with vague, never-diagnosed paroxysmal histories, that confirmation is sometimes the answer to a question they had given up on asking.
