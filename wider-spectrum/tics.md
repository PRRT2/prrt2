# Tics

A **tic** is a sudden, brief, repetitive movement or sound that the person did not consciously intend. Tics can be motor — eye blinking, facial movements, shoulder shrugs, head jerks — or vocal — throat clearing, sniffing, words, sounds. They commonly come and go, intensify with stress, and can sometimes be briefly suppressed.

Tics are best known as a feature of **Tourette syndrome** and the related chronic motor and vocal tic disorders. They have not, until now, been a part of the PRRT2 picture as the medical literature describes it. This page explores why we think they may belong in the conversation.

## What is known about tics

Tics are understood to arise from circuits connecting the **cortex, basal ganglia, and thalamus** — the same circuits that govern much of voluntary movement. When these circuits become hyperactive, or lose some of their normal restraint, sudden involuntary movements can break through. That much is established neurology.

What causes that loss of restraint in any given person is often unclear. Tourette syndrome is highly heritable, but it is not caused by a single gene; many genes appear to contribute, and environment plays a role too.

## Why a PRRT2 question arises

The basal-ganglia circuits that misfire in tic disorders are the same circuits implicated in paroxysmal dyskinesia. PRRT2 plays a recognized role in their stability — restraining neural excitability, modulating neurotransmitter release. A weakened brake on those circuits could, in principle, surface as tic-like behavior in some carriers, particularly given that tics — like other PRRT2 features — are **paroxysmal**: they come in bursts, with quiet periods in between.

This is not a claim that PRRT2 causes Tourette syndrome, or that tics are a PRRT2 disorder. It is the observation that the underlying circuitry and behavior pattern of tic disorders overlap meaningfully with what PRRT2 is known to disrupt.

## What the established literature does and does not say

The current formal literature **does not list tics among PRRT2-related conditions**. As with dysphonia, this is an absence — not a refutation, and not a presence.

## What PRRT2.org observes

Some carriers in our community describe tic-like phenomena — sudden, brief movements or vocalizations distinct from their paroxysmal kinesigenic episodes, and not adequately explained by them. These reports are informal and unstudied, but the overlap with both the established mechanism and the established phenotypes is striking enough that we believe the question is worth raising.

## If tic symptoms are part of your experience

A few practical notes if tics are part of what you live with:

* **Tic disorders are independently diagnosable and treatable**, regardless of any PRRT2 connection. A neurologist — particularly one who treats movement disorders — can evaluate them on their own terms.
* Some treatments used for related movement disorders, including certain sodium-channel-modulating medications, can occasionally help tic symptoms — though evidence in tic disorders specifically is separate from PRRT2.
* **Documenting timing, triggers, and the relationship to your other paroxysmal episodes** is useful both for your own care and for the broader effort to understand what PRRT2 may or may not do.
