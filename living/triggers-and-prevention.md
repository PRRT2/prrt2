# Triggers & Prevention

Because PRRT2 episodes are so often provoked rather than random, understanding *your* triggers — and building small habits around them — is one of the most practical forms of control available. This page focuses on the day-to-day, situational side of prevention. For the dietary and medical side, see [Lifestyle & Diet](../treatments/lifestyle-and-diet.md), which this page complements rather than repeats.

## Know your own triggers

Triggers vary from person to person, but common ones across PRRT2 conditions include:

* **Sudden movement or a startle** — the hallmark trigger for PKD. Beginning to walk, standing up quickly, being startled.
* **Sustained exertion** — the trigger for PED.
* **Stress and strong emotion** — among the most frequently reported, and one of the most modifiable.
* **Fatigue and disrupted sleep** — being run-down lowers the threshold for episodes.
* **Illness, fever, and overheating.**

The key word is *your*. General lists are a starting point; your own pattern is what matters, and [symptom tracking](../diagnosis/symptom-tracking.md) is how you find it. Patterns invisible day to day often jump out of a written record.

## Recognize your warning signs

Many people with PKD describe a brief **premonitory sensation** in the seconds before an episode — a tightening, a tingling, a "wave," often in the limb about to be affected. This warning is useful: it is a moment to **get safe** — to stop, sit, steady yourself, or ease out of whatever movement is beginning. Learning to recognize your own warning signs turns episodes from pure surprises into something you can sometimes brace for.

## Situational prevention

A few practical strategies that people find helpful:

* **Ease into movement.** For kinesigenic episodes, starting movement gradually — rather than launching abruptly from stillness — can reduce the trigger. Some people deliberately pause and "warm up" before transitions.
* **Pace exertion.** If sustained activity is a trigger, knowing your threshold lets you rest before reaching it.
* **Protect sleep and manage stress.** These are not vague wellness advice for a paroxysmal condition — they directly raise or lower your episode threshold.
* **Plan for high-risk moments.** If you know certain situations reliably provoke episodes, you can prepare: give yourself extra time, position yourself safely, or let a companion know.

## Prevention works alongside treatment, not instead of it

For people whose episodes are well controlled on medication, triggers matter much less — the medication has raised the threshold. Trigger management is most valuable when episodes are not fully controlled, while a treatment is being adjusted, or for the residual episodes that slip through. It is a complement to medical treatment, never a replacement for it. (See [How Treatment Works](../treatments/how-treatment-works.md).)

**🧭 Foundation Note**

_The PRRT2 Foundation’s own synthesis and lived experience, offered alongside the established science — not as established medical fact._

There is a quiet power in knowing your own triggers and warning signs. It does not cure anything, but it shifts the balance — from feeling at the mercy of your nervous system toward working *with* it. In our experience, the people who live most easily with PRRT2 are often those who have learned their own patterns well enough to anticipate them.

That knowledge comes from paying attention over time. It is, in the most practical sense, expertise — and you are the only person positioned to develop it about yourself.
