# For Caregivers

If someone you love has a PRRT2-related condition — a child, a partner, a parent, a sibling — you are part of how they live well with it. This page is for you: what to understand, what to do during an episode, and how to support without overreaching.

***

## Understand what the episodes are

The most reassuring thing to understand is what these episodes usually are — and are not. In PKD and the related paroxysmal movement disorders:

* Episodes are typically **brief** — often seconds to a minute.
* The person usually **remains fully conscious and aware** throughout, even if they cannot control the movement or speak in the moment.
* They are **self-limiting** — they stop on their own.

Knowing this changes how an episode feels to witness. What can look frightening is, in most cases, a brief and self-resolving event in someone who is still fully present.

Seizures have their own character and their own appropriate response, covered below. Understand your specific person's episodes by talking with them and their clinician — not by assuming that one PRRT2 presentation looks like another.

***

## What to do during an episode

### During a movement episode (PKD or PED)

For a typical brief, conscious movement episode, the most helpful response is calm and simple:

* **Stay calm.** Your calm helps them stay calm, which matters because stress can worsen or prolong episodes.
* **Keep them safe.** Help them to a stable position if they are at risk of falling; clear anything hazardous nearby.
* **Do not restrain them** or try to force the movement to stop. Let the episode run its short course.
* **Be present, not alarmed.** A reassuring presence is worth more than intervention.
* **Afterward,** let them recover at their own pace. Ask what they need rather than assuming.

### During a seizure

Seizure first aid follows standard principles:

* **Stay with them** and keep calm.
* **Time the seizure.** Most last under two minutes. If it continues past five minutes, call emergency services.
* **Keep them safe.** Ease them to the floor if possible, clear the area, and put something soft under their head.
* **Do not put anything in their mouth.** People cannot swallow their tongues.
* **Do not restrain them.**
* **After the seizure,** turn them onto their side (recovery position) if they are unconscious or drowsy, and stay with them while they recover.

If a seizure is **longer than five minutes**, if they do not regain consciousness, or if multiple seizures occur without recovery between them, call emergency services. Follow the specific emergency plan their neurologist or epileptologist has given you — PRRT2-related seizures in infants and children should always have a written plan from the treating clinician.

## For parents of an infant with BFIS

A new diagnosis of [Benign Familial Infantile Seizures](../conditions/bfis.md) carries an immediate emotional weight that the word "benign" does not fully soften. Watching your infant seize is frightening, full stop. The clinical picture is reassuring — but fear and reassurance can coexist, and both are legitimate.

A few things that matter most in the early period:

**Get to the right specialist quickly.** An infant with seizures needs a pediatric neurologist or epileptologist — ideally one with experience in genetic epilepsies. The sooner the PRRT2 connection is established and treatment started if needed, the better the short-term management. See [How to Get Tested](../diagnosis/how-to-get-tested.md).

**Understand what "self-limited" means in practice.** Most children with BFIS outgrow the seizures by around age two to three. Treatment during the active period is typically effective. This does not mean you wait and do nothing — it means the long-term prognosis is genuinely good, which is real information worth holding onto.

**Know the seizure pattern.** BFIS seizures typically come in clusters and are brief. Learning what your child's individual episodes look like — how they start, how long they last, how the child behaves afterward — helps you communicate clearly with clinicians and respond calmly.

**Keep the family history in the record.** PRRT2 is inherited. If your child has BFIS, a parent, sibling, or other relative may carry the same variant and may have had or may develop a different expression — movement episodes, migraine. That history belongs in everyone's medical record. See [Inheritance & Genetics](../overview/inheritance-and-genetics.md).

**Watch for the movement disorder later.** Children with BFIS have a chance of developing [PKD](../conditions/pkd.md) or [ICCA](../conditions/icca.md) in later childhood or adolescence. This is not inevitable, but it is worth knowing so that if it happens, it is recognized immediately rather than treated as a new unexplained problem.

## For caregivers of adults with PRRT2

Caring for an adult with PRRT2 — a partner, a parent, an adult child — involves a different set of dynamics. The medical management is usually their own responsibility. What caregivers provide is different: context, safety, advocacy, and partnership.

**Know their pattern.** Every person's episodes are different. Understanding what theirs look like — what triggers them, how they feel beforehand, how long they last, what helps afterward — makes you a better companion in an episode and a more effective advocate with clinicians.

**Respect their autonomy.** Adults with a paroxysmal condition are typically very good at managing their own bodies. They have usually been doing it for years, sometimes decades before a diagnosis. Your role is partnership, not management.

**Be an advocate in medical settings.** PRRT2 remains under-recognized. A caregiver who knows the condition and can speak to the history — including the family pattern, the prior misdiagnoses, the timeline — can be the difference between a productive appointment and another missed opportunity.

**Understand the late-diagnosis reality.** Many adults with PRRT2 went undiagnosed for years or decades. They may have accumulated other diagnoses, other explanations, other frameworks for understanding their body. A genetic diagnosis in adulthood reorganizes a lot of history. That reorganization can be a relief, a grief, or both simultaneously — and a caregiver who understands what that process feels like is a genuine support.

***

## Support without overprotecting

The instinct to protect is loving, but autonomy matters enormously to quality of life for someone with a paroxysmal condition. Aim to:

* **Follow their lead** on how much help they want. Many people manage their own condition well and need partnership, not supervision.
* **Avoid narrating or over-managing** their symptoms in front of others, which can be diminishing.
* **Respect that they are the expert** on their own body and episodes.

***

## Look after yourself too

Caregiving carries real emotional weight, especially with an unpredictable condition. Your wellbeing is not a luxury — it is part of what sustains your ability to support someone else. Seek your own support, whether through others who understand (see [Links & Support](../resources/links-and-support.md)), or simply naming the load you carry.

***

## Being an advocate

In medical settings, a caregiver who knows the condition can be a powerful advocate — especially given how rare and under-recognized PRRT2 is. Carrying the [For Your Doctor](../resources/for-your-doctor.md) summary, knowing the person's history, and speaking up when something is being missed are all genuine contributions.
