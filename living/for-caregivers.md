# For Caregivers

If someone you love has a PRRT2-related condition — a child, a partner, a parent, a sibling — you are part of how they live well with it. This page is for you: what to understand, what to do during an episode, and how to support without smothering.

## Understand what the episodes are

The most reassuring thing to understand is what these episodes usually are — and are not. In PKD and the related paroxysmal conditions:

* Episodes are typically **brief** — often seconds to a minute.
* In PKD, the person usually **remains fully conscious and aware** throughout, even if they cannot control the movement or speak in the moment.
* They are **self-limiting** — they stop on their own.

Knowing this changes how an episode feels to witness. What can look frightening is, in most cases, a brief and self-resolving event in someone who is still fully present.

(There is important nuance here: some people experience episodes involving loss of muscle tone, and the broader spectrum varies. Understand your specific person's episodes by talking with them and their clinician — not by assuming.)

## What to do during an episode

For the typical brief, conscious episode, the most helpful response is calm and simple:

* **Stay calm.** Your calm helps them stay calm, which matters because stress can prolong or worsen episodes.
* **Keep them safe.** Help them to a stable position if they are at risk of falling; clear anything hazardous nearby.
* **Do not restrain them** or try to force the movement to stop. Let the episode run its short course.
* **Be present, not alarmed.** A reassuring presence is worth more than intervention.
* **Afterward,** let them recover at their own pace. Ask what they need rather than assuming.

If an episode is ever very different from their usual pattern — much longer, with loss of consciousness, or otherwise alarming — that warrants medical attention, and you should follow the plan their clinician has given you.

## Support without overprotecting

The instinct to protect is loving, but autonomy matters enormously to quality of life. Aim to:

* **Follow their lead** on how much help they want. Many people manage their own condition well and need partnership, not supervision.
* **Avoid narrating or over-managing** their symptoms in front of others, which can be diminishing.
* **Respect that they are the expert** on their own body and episodes.

## For parents of a child with PRRT2

If your child has infantile seizures or childhood PKD, two messages matter most: the conditions are usually **highly treatable**, and many children **outgrow** the early symptoms. Your role is to ensure good specialist care, keep the family history in the record (it may matter for siblings or for your child later in life — see [Inheritance & Genetics](../overview/inheritance-and-genetics.md)), and let your child be a child.

## Look after yourself too

Caregiving carries real emotional weight, especially with an unpredictable condition. Your wellbeing is not a luxury — it is part of what sustains your ability to support someone else. Seek your own support, whether through others who understand (see [Links & Support](../resources/links-and-support.md)), respite, or simply naming the load you carry.

## Being an advocate

In medical settings, a caregiver who knows the condition can be a powerful advocate — especially given how rare and under-recognized PRRT2 is. Carrying the [For Your Doctor](../resources/for-your-doctor.md) summary, knowing the person's history, and speaking up when something is being missed are all genuine contributions.
