# Atypical Epilepsy Phenotypes

PRRT2-associated epilepsy was initially defined by its most recognizable presentation: benign, self-limited infantile seizures that resolve before age two. That characterization remains accurate for the majority of cases. But as genetic testing has expanded and case series have accumulated, a second picture has emerged — one of atypical presentations, persistent seizures, and a population of adults whose epilepsy history predates both their genetic diagnosis and the discovery of the gene itself.

***

## Established

### Recognized atypical presentations

Research has documented PRRT2 epilepsy phenotypes that fall outside the classic BFIS pattern, including:

- **Atypical BFIS courses** — neonatal seizure onset, prolonged active epilepsy beyond age two, and recurrence of seizures in adulthood
- **Febrile seizures and febrile seizures plus (FS+)** — PRRT2 variants have been identified in some patients with febrile seizure syndromes
- **Focal epilepsy** — including adolescent-onset focal epilepsy without prior infantile seizure history, and drug-resistant focal epilepsy in rare cases
- **Severe phenotypes** — developmental and epileptic encephalopathy, status epilepticus during sleep, and epileptic spasms have been reported in a minority of patients, particularly those with homozygous or compound heterozygous variants
- **Adult-onset recurrence** — recurrent seizures in adulthood following an atypical or prolonged early course

A 2014 study of five Spanish families with confirmed PRRT2-related BFIS found that 36% of mutation carriers presented with a non-BFIS phenotype or an atypical clinical course, including learning difficulties and recurrent seizures in adulthood. The authors concluded that these findings expand the clinical spectrum of PRRT2 mutations to include non-benign epileptic phenotypes. [(Guerrero-López et al., *Epilepsy Research*, 2014 — PMID 25060993)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25060993/)

### Mechanism and variation

PRRT2 regulates presynaptic neurotransmitter release and modulates voltage-gated sodium channels. The variability in clinical expression — why one mutation carrier has self-limited infantile seizures while another has persistent adult epilepsy — is not yet well understood. Factors under investigation include variant type, zygosity (heterozygous versus homozygous), and age-dependent expression of PRRT2 across different brain regions.

***

## Emerging

### Recent literature expanding the phenotype

A 2024 literature review in *Seizure* identified PRRT2-associated epileptic phenotypes ranging from benign medication-responsive forms to more severe epileptic encephalopathies, including absence seizures, non-convulsive seizures, and developmental epileptic encephalopathy with spike-wave activation in sleep. Drug-resistant epilepsy was observed in a subset of patients. [(Exploring epileptic phenotypes in PRRT2-related disorders, *Seizure*, 2024)](https://www.seizure-journal.com/article/S1059-1311(24)00124-9/fulltext)

A 2025 international cohort study in *Neurology Genetics* confirmed that self-limited infantile epilepsy remains the dominant PRRT2 epilepsy phenotype, while also identifying atypical presentations including adolescent-onset focal epilepsy without prior infantile seizure history. The study explicitly noted that adolescent- and adult-onset focal epilepsy without a prior infantile seizure history is "not well described" in the PRRT2 literature, and that recurrent seizures in adulthood following an atypical early course have "rarely been described" — noting the absence of data rather than the absence of cases. [(Komar et al., *Neurology Genetics*, 2025 — PMID 40401013)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40401013/)

A 2024 study of adult patients with PRRT2-related epilepsy found seizure onset distributed across infancy, adolescence, and adulthood, with epilepsy persisting or relapsing into adulthood in all reported cases. Epileptiform discharges were recorded in the majority of these adult patients — a finding that diverges from the normal interictal EEG typically associated with classic BFIS. Reduced thalamocortical connectivity on fMRI was also documented, indicating measurable network-level disruption that standard clinical evaluation does not routinely assess. [(Clinical and neuroimaging features of PRRT2-related epilepsy in adult patients, 2024 — PMID 41554218)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41554218/)

### The adult detection gap

Most PRRT2 epilepsy research has been conducted in pediatric populations, where the classic BFIS presentation is most recognizable and most frequently tested. PRRT2 testing in adults with unexplained or refractory epilepsy is not standard clinical practice. This creates a structural gap in the data: the prevalence of PRRT2-related epilepsy in adults — including those with persistent seizures, atypical presentations, or late-onset recurrence — has not been formally studied.

Genetic epilepsy panels are increasingly accessible, but their use in adult neurology requires a clinical environment that considers PRRT2 as a candidate beyond the pediatric setting. That environment does not yet exist systematically.

***

{% hint style="info" %}
**Foundation Note**

PRRT2 was first identified as a disease gene in 2011. That is a recent discovery by any clinical standard — and it means there is a specific population whose epilepsy history predates the gene entirely: adults who received their diagnoses before PRRT2 was known, whose workups were thorough by the standards of the time, and who were never tested for something that did not yet exist as a clinical entity.

The literature is beginning to document that PRRT2-associated epilepsy is not always self-limited, not always infantile in onset, and not always mild. But those descriptions remain early-stage — case series, small cohorts, individual reports. The absence of large adult data is not evidence that adults are unaffected. It reflects the fact that adults have not been systematically studied.

PRRT2 appears on commercial epilepsy genetic panels for a reason. Expanded genetic testing access — and a clinical culture that asks "why?" about unexplained epilepsy rather than only "what medication?" — would reach a population the current research has not yet counted. The Foundation's position is that PRRT2 testing should be part of the conversation for any adult with unexplained or treatment-resistant epilepsy, particularly where there is a personal or family history of seizures, movement disorders, or both.

Until systematic testing reaches adults, the true scope of PRRT2-related epilepsy in that population remains unknown. That is not a gap the existing literature can fill. It is one that awareness, advocacy, and access can.
