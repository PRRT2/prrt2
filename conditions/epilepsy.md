# Epilepsy — Beyond BFIS

PRRT2 is a gene. When it carries a mutation, one of the things it can cause is seizures — and the most common form by far is seizures that start in the first year of life and stop on their own before age two.

If that is why you are here, you are in the right place, but this is not the page you need first.

***

## What "BFIS" actually means

BFIS is the abbreviation you will see everywhere in PRRT2 material, usually without anyone explaining it. Taken apart, it is four ordinary words:

| Letter | Word      | What it means in plain language                                                                                                     |
| ------ | --------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **B**  | Benign    | Expected to go away on its own, and expected to leave no lasting harm. It says nothing about how frightening it is to live through. |
| **F**  | Familial  | It runs in families. It is inherited, not caused by anything that happened during pregnancy, birth, or since.                       |
| **I**  | Infantile | It affects babies and infants — the first year of life, most often around **4 to 7 months old**.                                    |
| **S**  | Seizures  | Also called **convulsions**, **fits**, or **spells**, depending on who is talking and when they trained.                            |

So **BFIS = inherited seizures in babies that are expected to stop.**

You may also see these names on paperwork. They describe the same condition:

* **BFIE** — benign familial infantile **epilepsy**
* **BFIC** — benign familial infantile **convulsions**
* **SeLIE** — self-limited (familial) infantile epilepsy — **this is the current preferred term**
* **Benign partial epilepsy in infancy** — an older term
* **Infantile convulsions** — the everyday phrase many parents and older clinicians use
* **Fukuyama-Watanabe-Vigevano syndrome** — after the clinicians who first described it

In 2017 the International League Against Epilepsy replaced the word _benign_ with **self-limited**, and reaffirmed that in its 2022 classification of infant epilepsy syndromes. The reasoning was straightforward: _self-limited_ describes what actually happens — the seizures stop on their own timeline — without implying that watching your baby convulse is a mild experience.

If your neurologist says BFIS and your genetic report says SeLIE, they mean the same thing. Details on all of it are on the [**BFIS / SeLIE page**](bfis.md).

***

## What "beyond BFIS" means

Within PRRT2-associated epilepsy, the infant form described above accounts for roughly **nine out of ten cases** in the published literature. That is the well-lit part of the map.

"Beyond BFIS" is everything else:

* seizures in a baby that **do not stop** by age two
* seizures that **start later** — in childhood, adolescence, or adulthood — sometimes with no infant seizures at all
* seizures that are **harder to control** than the reassuring description predicts
* the **adults** who had unexplained seizures decades before this gene was discovered, and were never tested

None of that replaces the favorable picture. Most PRRT2 families genuinely do get the good version. But if your family is in the other tenth, you should not have to find that out by noticing you are missing from the reassuring page — so this is where it is written down.

***

## Established

### The mechanism, in one paragraph

The PRRT2 gene builds a protein that sits at the synapse — the junction where one brain cell passes a signal to the next. Its job is partly to act as a **brake**: it helps regulate how much neurotransmitter gets released, and it dampens the sodium channels that make neurons fire. When one copy of the gene is faulty, that brake weakens, and neurons fire more easily than they should. Seizures are one result. Sudden involuntary movements are another. Migraine is another. **One weak brake, in different brain circuits, at different ages** — that is why a single gene produces such a scattered-looking list of conditions. See [How the PRRT2 Gene Works](../overview/how-the-gene-works.md) and [The Sodium Channel Connection](../overview/sodium-channel-connection.md).

PRRT2 is included on commercial epilepsy genetic panels run by major diagnostic laboratories. That is not a formality — it reflects consensus that a pathogenic PRRT2 variant changes how a case is understood, what the outlook is, and which class of medication tends to work.

### Primary phenotype: seizures in babies

The most common PRRT2-associated epilepsy is [BFIS / SeLIE](bfis.md), a self-limited autosomal dominant syndrome. In the largest published analysis of PRRT2 cases — 1,444 reported patients — BFIE was the single largest diagnostic group in the entire PRRT2 spectrum at **41.7% (n = 602)**, ahead of paroxysmal kinesigenic dyskinesia at 38.7% (n = 560) and ICCA at 14.3% (n = 206), with only 5.3% (n = 76) carrying a different primary diagnosis. Within PRRT2-associated **epilepsy** specifically, BFIE is described in the literature as accounting for approximately **90% of cases**. [(Ebrahimi-Fakhari et al., _Brain_, 2015 — PMID 26598493)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26598493/) [(Scorrano et al., _Seizure_, 2024 — PMID 38749256)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38749256/)

What that looks like in practice, in plain terms:

* Seizures begin in the **first year of life** — most often around **4 to 7 months**, with published onset ranging from roughly 3 to 20 months.
* They usually arrive in **clusters** — several in a day, repeating every few hours, then stopping — rather than as one isolated event.
* Individual seizures are **brief**, typically under five minutes.
* They are usually **not caused by fever**, which distinguishes them from ordinary febrile seizures.
* What a parent sees can range from a pause and a stare, eyes pulling to one side, or a change in breathing or colour, through to whole-body stiffening and rhythmic jerking.
* **Brain MRI and the between-seizure EEG are expected to be normal.** In this diagnosis, "normal" is not a failure to find the problem — it is part of the diagnosis.
* Seizures **resolve in the majority of children before age two**, and development is expected to be typical.

Medication is often still needed, because the seizure burden in that first year can be too high to watch and wait. Sodium channel–blocking medications are the class described as effective, and seizure freedom is achieved in most treated infants. Which specific medication suits your child is a decision for your pediatric neurologist — see [Treatment for BFIS](../treatments/by-condition/bfis.md) and [How Treatment Works for PRRT2](../treatments/how-treatment-works.md).

**For most families with a PRRT2 mutation and infant seizures, the trajectory is favorable.** The full detail — clusters, medication evidence, prognosis, developmental monitoring, how to get the right referral — is on the [**BFIS / SeLIE page**](bfis.md).

### The broader spectrum

BFIS is not the full extent of PRRT2-associated epilepsy. Research has established that a subset of PRRT2 mutation carriers present with epilepsy that does not resolve in infancy — including atypical courses, focal epilepsy, drug-resistant presentations, and recurrent seizures in adulthood. A 2014 study of five confirmed PRRT2-BFIS families found that **36% of mutation carriers had atypical or non-BFIS presentations**, including recurrent seizures in adulthood. [(Guerrero-López et al., _Epilepsy Research_, 2014 — PMID 25060993)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25060993/)

Those figures measure different things and are not in conflict. The 41.7% and \~90% figures above count **published patients grouped by primary diagnosis** — they describe how often BFIE is the diagnosis on record. The 36% figure counts **variant carriers within known BFIS families**, including relatives identified by cascade testing rather than by presenting to a clinic. Carrier-based counts consistently surface more atypical presentations, because they capture people who would never have been written up as a BFIE case in the first place.

## Beyond BFIS, in detail

Each of the following is documented in the literature. Together they are the case for treating PRRT2 as an epilepsy gene across the lifespan rather than an infancy-only diagnosis.

### 1. Seizures that continue past age two

Uncommon, but documented. In one cohort of PRRT2 infants, three of 36 children needed medication for recurrent seizures after age three — with recurrences at ages five, seven, and ten and a half. All were brought under control. [(Döring et al., _Biomedicines_, 2020 — PMID 33126500)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33126500/)

If your child is past the age where the seizures were supposed to have stopped, that does not mean the diagnosis is wrong. It means your child is in the part of the range that the summary paragraphs skip.

### 2. Seizures that begin in the newborn period

Onset in the first weeks of life is unusual for this condition but reported. In one series, newborn-onset infants had their last seizures before six months and developed normally — the same favorable course, on an earlier clock. [(Lee et al., _Epilepsia Open_, 2023 — PMID 36775847)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36775847/)

### 3. Fever-related seizures

PRRT2 variants have been identified in some patients with **febrile seizures** and **febrile seizures plus (FS+)** — seizure syndromes triggered or accompanied by fever. Classic PRRT2 infant seizures are characteristically _without_ fever, so a fever-associated pattern is one of the ways a PRRT2 case can look unfamiliar. Current guidance recommends treating fevers promptly in children with this diagnosis.

### 4. Seizures that start in adolescence or adulthood

A 2025 international cohort study confirmed that self-limited infant epilepsy remains the dominant PRRT2 epilepsy phenotype, while also identifying **adolescent-onset focal epilepsy in people with no prior history of infant seizures**. The authors were explicit that this pattern is "not well described" in the PRRT2 literature, and that recurrent seizures in adulthood following an atypical early course have "rarely been described" — language that records an absence of data, not an absence of cases. [(Komar et al., _Neurology Genetics_, 2025 — PMID 40401013)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40401013/)

### 5. Focal epilepsy and seizures that resist medication

**Focal** means the seizure starts in one region of the brain rather than everywhere at once. It is the usual pattern in PRRT2 infant seizures, and it also turns up as an ongoing focal epilepsy in older patients — including, in a minority, epilepsy that does not respond well to medication. A 2024 literature review identified PRRT2-associated presentations ranging from mild and medication-responsive through to more severe forms, with drug-resistant epilepsy in a subset of patients. [(Scorrano et al., _Seizure_, 2024 — PMID 38749256)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38749256/)

### 6. Severe presentations

Reported in a minority of patients, and concentrated among those carrying variants in **both** copies of the gene rather than one: developmental and epileptic encephalopathy, epileptic spasms, absence and non-convulsive seizures, and status epilepticus during sleep. Two-copy (biallelic) variants are seen in under 1% of people with PRRT2 variants and carry a different and more serious clinical picture than the single-copy diagnosis most of this site describes.

Separately, if a genetic report says **16p11.2 deletion** rather than a PRRT2 sequence variant, that is a related but distinct diagnosis — PRRT2 sits inside a stretch of chromosome 16 that can be deleted along with neighbouring genes. Both situations are explained on the [BFIS page](bfis.md#when-it-doesnt-follow-the-script).

### 7. Measurable differences in adults that routine testing misses

A 2024 study of adults with PRRT2-related epilepsy found seizure onset spread across infancy, adolescence, and adulthood, with epilepsy persisting or relapsing into adulthood in all reported cases. **Epileptiform discharges were recorded in the majority of these adults** — a departure from the normal between-seizure EEG associated with classic infant PRRT2 epilepsy. Reduced thalamocortical connectivity was also documented on functional MRI, indicating network-level disruption that standard clinical evaluation does not routinely look for. [(PMID 41554218)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41554218/)

### 8. The adults nobody tested

PRRT2 was identified as a disease gene in **2011**. Every adult whose epilepsy was worked up before then was investigated thoroughly by the standards of the time — for something that did not yet exist as a clinical entity.

Most PRRT2 epilepsy research since has been done in children, where the classic infant pattern is most recognizable and most often tested. PRRT2 testing in adults with unexplained or treatment-resistant epilepsy is **not standard clinical practice**. The result is a structural hole in the data: nobody has formally measured how common PRRT2-related epilepsy is in adults. The absence of large adult datasets is not evidence that adults are unaffected — it reflects the fact that adults have not been systematically studied.

### 9. Why the same variant looks different in different people

Family members carrying the **identical** PRRT2 variant routinely have different presentations — one with infant seizures only, one with movement episodes only, one with both, one with nothing at all. There are no established genotype–phenotype correlations, meaning the specific variant on a report does not currently predict which symptoms will appear or when. Factors under investigation include variant type, whether one or both copies are affected, and how PRRT2 expression changes across brain regions with age. See [Inheritance & Genetics](../overview/inheritance-and-genetics.md).

## Why the genetic answer changes something

Parents sometimes ask what a gene name is actually good for once the seizures are controlled. Three concrete things:

1. **It informs which class of medication is tried first.** Because the underlying problem is a weakened brake on sodium channels, sodium channel–blocking medications are the class most consistently described as effective in PRRT2 epilepsy — and the published cohorts show a real difference in first-medication success. That is a conversation for your neurologist, and the evidence is laid out on [Treatment for Epilepsy](../treatments/by-condition/epilepsy.md) and [How Treatment Works for PRRT2](../treatments/how-treatment-works.md).
2. **It tells you what else to watch for, without alarm.** The same variant that caused seizures in infancy can later show up as brief movement episodes triggered by sudden motion — see [PKD](pkd.md) and [ICCA](icca.md) — or as [hemiplegic migraine](hemiplegic-migraine.md). Knowing that turns a confusing new symptom at age nine into an answered question in one appointment.
3. **It explains the family history.** The aunt with unexplained clumsy spells, the grandparent's childhood convulsions nobody recorded, the cousin with migraines and weakness. About 39% of PRRT2 cases look sporadic with no family history at all — usually because carriers can be entirely unaffected, or because the history is there and was never connected. [(Döring et al., 2020 — PMID 33126500)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33126500/)

## Emerging

The PRRT2 epilepsy phenotype spectrum is under active investigation. Recent literature has expanded the recognized range beyond BFIS to include presentations in adolescents and adults, cases of drug-resistant epilepsy, and neuroimaging findings suggesting network-level disruption not detectable through standard clinical evaluation. The adult phenotype in particular remains poorly characterized — not because it is rare, but because systematic research in adult populations has not yet been conducted.

Genetic epilepsy panels are increasingly accessible. Their use in adult neurology, however, requires a clinical environment that considers PRRT2 a candidate outside the pediatric setting — and that environment does not yet exist systematically.

Current literature is summarized in [Atypical Presentations](atypical-epilepsy.md) and [Current Literature](../research/current-literature.md).

## Questions worth bringing to your appointment

* Is my child's seizure pattern consistent with the **self-limited infant form**, or with something outside it?
* Do we have a **written seizure action plan**, including rescue medication and when to call emergency services?
* Are you a neurologist with expertise in **both epilepsy and paroxysmal movement disorders** — and if not, who should we be seeing?
* Was this a PRRT2 **sequence variant** or a **16p11.2 deletion**?
* Can we have the **variant name in writing** for our records?
* What movement or migraine symptoms in later childhood should bring us back to you?
* _(For adults)_ Given my seizure history and family history, is a **genetic epilepsy panel** appropriate?

[For Your Doctor](../resources/for-your-doctor.md) is written to be handed to a clinician who has not encountered this gene before.

**🧭 Foundation Note**

_The PRRT2 Foundation's own synthesis and lived experience, offered alongside the established science — not as established medical fact._

PRRT2 was identified as a disease gene in 2011. The clinical literature built around it since has focused overwhelmingly on the most visible presentation — infant seizures that resolve — and much less on what happens when they do not, or when a person was already an adult by the time the gene was discovered.

There is a quiet cost to a diagnosis labeled benign and self-resolving: it gets closed out. The chart moves on. Fifteen years later a teenager develops brief movement episodes triggered by standing up too fast, and nobody connects it to seizures that stopped before kindergarten — because everyone was told that chapter was finished. That is how PRRT2 families end up doing the diagnostic journey twice.

So we would ask two things of you, and they are not in tension.

**Believe the favorable prognosis.** It is real, it is well documented, and if your baby has the common form of this condition the odds are strongly in your child's favour.

**And keep the file.** The variant name, the age of onset, what the seizures looked like, what worked. Tell your pediatrician to keep it in the active record rather than the resolved-history column. Tell your child, when they are old enough, that they carry a PRRT2 variant and what that can mean at different ages.

Not because something bad is coming. Because if anything does appear later, you will have handed your child the one thing most adults with this gene never got: an answer that was already waiting.

And if your child is the one whose seizures did not stop, or who has a developmental concern, or a two-copy variant, or a 16p11.2 deletion — you are not a failure of the statistics and you are not outside this community. The published literature skews toward the children who fit the pattern, because those are the children who get written up as confirming it. The atypical cases are underrepresented, not absent. Closing that gap is one of the reasons this Foundation and its [patient registry](https://intake.prrt2.org/) exist.
{% endhint %}
