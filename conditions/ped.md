# Paroxysmal Exertional Dyskinesia (PED)

Paroxysmal exertional dyskinesia is a movement disorder in which episodes of involuntary movement — typically dystonia, chorea, or a combination — are brought on by sustained physical activity. Unlike the brief, movement-triggered episodes of PKD, PED episodes arise after prolonged exertion and tend to last longer, usually settling with rest.

PED has historically been associated most strongly with a different gene — SLC2A1, which causes GLUT1 deficiency syndrome. But the picture is more complex than early classification suggested, and the evidence that PRRT2 can also cause or contribute to exertional episodes is substantial.

## PRRT2 as a cause of PED

The association between PRRT2 and exertional episodes is documented in peer-reviewed literature across multiple independent reports. A published family case study identified a PRRT2 nonsense mutation in a family showing overlapping phenotypes across all three paroxysmal dyskinesia subtypes — including exercise-triggered episodes — and the authors specifically noted that the exercise trigger in PRRT2 carriers "likely represents an underestimated occurrence." A direct genetic study of Chinese families with paroxysmal dyskinesia confirmed PRRT2 mutations in cases presenting with exercise-induced phenotypes, explicitly testing and supporting the hypothesis that PRRT2 causes PED and PNKD forms as well as PKD. A 2018 clinical case report documents a PRRT2 carrier whose primary complaint was exertional hand dystonia and limb tightness occurring specifically during running sessions. A 2023 review article in *Neurology* lists PRRT2 among the genes that can cause PED presentations, noting that such cases often carry "additional phenomenology" beyond a pure PED picture.

Further supporting this, a major 2021 spectrum review found that approximately 40% of PRRT2 patients report additional non-kinesigenic triggers beyond sudden movement — including sustained exercise — demonstrating that exertional triggering is a recognized feature of PRRT2 disorders, not an anomaly.

## Why PRRT2-PED is likely underrecognized

The current classification of paroxysmal dyskinesias was largely built before the genetics were known. When patients were grouped by trigger type, the assumption was that kinesigenic, non-kinesigenic, and exertional phenotypes each had their own distinct gene. That turned out to be incorrect, and the categories still distort how PRRT2-exertional cases are counted.

When a PRRT2 carrier experiences both sudden-movement triggers and exertional triggers — which the literature confirms is common — they tend to be classified as "PKD with additional triggers" rather than counted separately as a PED case. This classification bias means the true frequency of exertional triggering in PRRT2 carriers is probably higher than published case counts reflect.

The literature itself notes this: the exercise trigger in PRRT2 has been explicitly described as "an underestimated occurrence," and recent expert reviews call for moving beyond trigger-type classification alone toward a framework that includes attack duration and medication response.

The under-classification of PRRT2-PED shows up in a pattern some in this community recognize: spending years with exertional episodes that were attributed to something else. One carrier known to this site had a childhood and adult history of what was called exercise-induced asthma — breathlessness, chest involvement, and loss of function during sustained activity. Connecting those events to PRRT2 and understanding them as exertional dystonic episodes, including laryngeal and respiratory muscle involvement, only became possible after genetic diagnosis in adulthood.

This kind of misattribution is not unusual in PRRT2. The exertional pattern, particularly when it involves the voice or breathing, can look like pulmonary, cardiac, or anxiety-driven phenomena before anyone considers a movement disorder gene. If that experience resonates, it matters for your medical history and for the conversations you have with your team.

The PED classification issue also matters practically: if exertional episodes are counted as "atypical PKD" rather than "PRRT2-PED," their frequency, severity, and treatment needs can be systematically underdocumented in the literature — and in individual medical records.

## How PED presents in PRRT2

When exertional episodes occur in PRRT2 carriers, they generally share features with PRRT2 paroxysmal episodes more broadly: they involve dystonic or choreiform movements, consciousness is maintained throughout, and there is typically a return to normal between episodes. They may affect whichever part of the body was engaged in exertion, or they may be more generalized.

A key distinguishing feature from GLUT1-related PED is the broader clinical context: PRRT2 carriers often have other features of the PRRT2 spectrum — a history of infantile seizures, sudden-movement triggers, vocal symptoms, or related family history — that are not part of the GLUT1 picture.

## How PRRT2-PED differs from GLUT1-PED

| | **PRRT2-PED** | **GLUT1-PED** |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanism** | Channelopathy / synaptopathy — impaired sodium channel regulation | Transportopathy — glucose cannot cross the blood-brain barrier efficiently |
| **Gene** | PRRT2 | SLC2A1 |
| **Other features** | Often co-occurs with PKD, infantile seizures, or other PRRT2 spectrum features | May include epilepsy, intellectual disability, or hemolytic anemia |
| **Trigger** | Sustained exertion; may also have kinesigenic triggers | Sustained exertion; fasting or low carbohydrate intake worsens episodes |
| **CSF glucose** | Normal | Low (below 60 mg/dL; ratio to blood glucose below 0.45–0.50) |
| **Ketogenic diet** | No established role; mechanistic plausibility (see Treatment page) | Often highly effective — first-line intervention |
| **Response to sodium-channel medications** | Possible; less consistent than in pure PKD | Generally not effective |

## Why genetic diagnosis matters here

The distinction between PRRT2 and GLUT1 is not just academic — the treatments diverge sharply. GLUT1-related PED responds to a ketogenic diet in a way that is mechanism-specific: the diet provides ketone bodies as an alternative fuel that bypasses the broken glucose transporter. Sodium-channel medications, which work well in PRRT2-PKD, are generally not effective in GLUT1-PED.

Conversely, while the evidence for any single treatment in PRRT2-PED is still building, the treatment logic is different from the outset. Testing only one gene when the clinical picture is ambiguous risks applying the wrong strategy.
