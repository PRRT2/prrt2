# Infantile Convulsions with Choreoathetosis (ICCA)

ICCA is the name for a specific pattern: infantile seizures and a paroxysmal movement disorder occurring in the same person or family. It is not a third, separate disease. It is what the PRRT2 spectrum looks like when two of its expressions appear together — and it was the clinical observation that first suggested a shared genetic root long before the gene was known.

***

## Established

### What ICCA describes

ICCA stands for **Infantile Convulsions and Choreoathetosis**. In practice, the two components are:

- **The seizure component** — identical to [Benign Familial Infantile Seizures (BFIS / SeLIE)](bfis.md): clusters of focal seizures beginning in the first year of life, typically self-limited by age two.
- **The movement disorder component** — identical to [Paroxysmal Kinesigenic Dyskinesia (PKD)](pkd.md): sudden, brief involuntary movements triggered by sudden motion, emerging in childhood or adolescence.

The name "choreoathetosis" reflects the terminology used when ICCA was first described in 1997, before the nature of the movement episodes was fully characterized. The movement component is now understood to be PKD.

### History: the clue before the gene

ICCA was first described in a French family in 1997 by Szepetowski and colleagues, who mapped it to chromosome 16p12–q12. [(Szepetowski et al., *Nature Genetics*, 1997 — PMID 9462745)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9462745/) At the time, PKD and BFIS were treated as separate conditions. The existence of ICCA — in which both appeared in the same individual or across generations of the same family — strongly implied a shared genetic cause. That cause was confirmed in 2011 and 2012 when PRRT2 mutations were identified as responsible for PKD, BFIS, and ICCA alike. [(Heron et al., *American Journal of Human Genetics*, 2012 — PMID 22243965)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22243965/)

ICCA is, in that sense, the condition that cracked the spectrum open.

### Genetics and presentation

Like BFIS and PKD, ICCA follows an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, and the same common PRRT2 mutation — c.649dupC — is found in the majority of ICCA families. PRRT2 mutations are identified in over 90% of ICCA families studied.

The two components do not always appear in the same generation. In some families, one member has the infantile seizures and another has the movement disorder; in others, the same person has both. The seizures typically come first, in infancy, and resolve. The PKD component typically emerges years later in childhood or adolescence. The gap between them — sometimes a decade or more — is part of why the connection was not obvious without genetic testing.

### Why ICCA matters clinically

An ICCA diagnosis carries practical implications beyond confirming the gene. If a child has infantile seizures and a parent or sibling has a movement disorder, or if a child who had infantile seizures develops involuntary movements in adolescence, ICCA is the framework that connects those events. Without it, they may be worked up separately, by different specialists, without anyone recognizing the thread.

PRRT2 testing is the diagnostic step that makes ICCA visible. [(GeneReviews — PRRT2-Related Disorder)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK475803/) [(OMIM 602066)](https://omim.org/entry/602066)

## Emerging

### ICCA and the broader epilepsy spectrum

Most ICCA research was conducted before the atypical end of the PRRT2 epilepsy spectrum was well characterized. The seizure component in ICCA has generally been assumed to follow the BFIS pattern — self-limited, benign, resolving by age two. Whether ICCA families carry the same risk of atypical or persistent epilepsy as those with isolated PRRT2 epilepsy is not yet well studied.

The movement disorder component of ICCA is well characterized and responds well to treatment. See [Treatment for PKD](../treatments/by-condition/pkd.md).

### Variable expression within ICCA families

Within a single ICCA family, the same PRRT2 variant can produce different combinations of expression: one person with BFIS only, another with PKD only, another with both, and potentially another with no symptoms at all. This is consistent with the variable expressivity observed across the PRRT2 spectrum generally, but the mechanisms governing which component appears in whom remain under investigation.

***

{% hint style="info" %}
**Foundation Note**

ICCA is an important condition for a reason that goes beyond clinical description: it is the most direct evidence that what was once categorized as separate diseases is a single genetic spectrum. The person who had infantile seizures that resolved and then developed a movement disorder a decade later was not unlucky twice. They had one gene expressing at different times.

That reframing matters for patients and families navigating a PRRT2 diagnosis. It explains why the family history looks the way it does — why a grandparent had one thing and a child has another, why siblings can present so differently. The gene is consistent even when the expression is not.

ICCA also stands as a model for what genetic diagnosis can do: it converted a medical puzzle that had been unsolved for decades into something nameable, inheritable, and treatable.
{% endhint %}
