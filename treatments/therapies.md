# Therapies

Medication is the centerpiece of treatment for the paroxysmal PRRT2 conditions, but it is not the whole picture. A range of **non-drug therapies** support function, comfort, and wellbeing — sometimes alongside medication, sometimes for the parts of life that medication does not reach.

## Physical therapy

Physical therapy can help in several ways: maintaining strength and mobility, working on balance and gait where movement episodes or dystonia have affected them, and building confidence in movement. For people whose episodes are triggered by sudden movement, a physical therapist can also help develop **movement strategies** — ways of initiating activity that are less likely to provoke an episode.

## Occupational therapy

Occupational therapy focuses on the practical business of daily life — adapting tasks, environments, and routines so that symptoms interfere as little as possible with work, self-care, and the things a person wants to do. For variable, paroxysmal conditions, this kind of practical problem-solving can be quietly transformative.

## Speech-language therapy

For voice and speech symptoms — including the dysphonia discussed in [Dysphonia](../wider-spectrum/dysphonia.md) — a **speech-language pathologist** provides voice therapy: techniques to use the voice more efficiently and comfortably, often used alongside medical treatments such as [botulinum toxin](medications/botulinum-toxin.md) for spasmodic dysphonia. Speech therapy can also help where speech is affected during or between episodes.

## Psychological support

Living with a paroxysmal condition carries a real emotional weight that deserves acknowledgment in its own right. The unpredictability of episodes, the experience of being misdiagnosed or disbelieved for years, the strain of managing a chronic condition — these are heavy. Psychological support, whether counseling, therapy, or peer support, is a legitimate and valuable part of care, not a sign that the condition is "all in your head." (The opposite is true: a confirmed genetic diagnosis is precisely what shows it is not.)

It is also worth noting that **stress is a common trigger** for PRRT2 episodes, so support that helps manage stress can have a double benefit — emotional and physical.

## Bringing it together

The most effective care often combines several of these: medication to control episodes, targeted therapy for specific problems, and practical and emotional support for living well. A specialist can help coordinate referrals to the right therapists.

> Informational only. The right combination of therapies is individual and best arranged through your care team.
