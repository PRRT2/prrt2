# Treatment for PRRT2-Associated Epilepsy

PRRT2-associated epilepsy covers a range of presentations, and treatment varies with them. At one end is [Benign Familial Infantile Seizures (BFIS)](../../conditions/bfis.md) — self-limited, typically short-course, good outcomes. At the other end are atypical presentations: persistent epilepsy, adult-onset seizures, and drug-resistant cases. Most of the formal treatment guidance was built around the typical end. This page covers both.

***

## Established

### Treatment for self-limited infantile epilepsy (BFIS)

The most common PRRT2 epilepsy presentation responds well to treatment. The standard approach:

**First-line medication: sodium channel blockers.** Carbamazepine is the most widely used and best-studied option. Oxcarbazepine is commonly used as an alternative. Both work by stabilizing neuronal membranes and reducing the hyperexcitability that drives PRRT2-related seizures. See [Carbamazepine](../medications/carbamazepine.md) for detailed prescribing context.

**Seizure control is typically achievable.** Studies of PRRT2-positive infantile epilepsy consistently report high rates of seizure freedom with sodium channel blockers — most treated patients achieve control with a single agent. [(Döring et al., *Neurology Genetics*, 2022 — PMID 36185376)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36185376/)

**Duration of treatment is usually limited.** Because BFIS is self-limiting — most children outgrow the seizures by age two to three — treatment is typically short-course. Decisions about stopping medication are made by the treating clinician as the child develops and remains seizure-free.

**Normal development is the expected outcome.** BFIS does not typically affect cognitive or developmental trajectory. The seizures, treated or untreated, do not leave structural damage in the typical case.

For families with infants in the BFIS presentation, see also [Treatment for BFIS](bfis.md) for additional context on the family-level considerations.

### The mechanism behind treatment response

PRRT2 mutations cause haploinsufficiency — reduced PRRT2 protein — which increases neuronal excitability partly through dysregulation of voltage-gated sodium channels. Sodium channel blockers address this downstream effect directly, which is why they work consistently across PRRT2-related seizures and movement disorders alike. This shared mechanism is discussed in [The Sodium Channel Connection](../../overview/sodium-channel-connection.md).

## Emerging

### Treatment for atypical and persistent PRRT2 epilepsy

The atypical end of the PRRT2 epilepsy spectrum — persistent seizures, drug-resistant focal epilepsy, adult-onset or recurrent seizures — presents a treatment challenge the literature has not yet fully addressed. Formal treatment guidelines for atypical PRRT2 epilepsy do not exist. What the available evidence shows:

**Sodium channel blockers remain first-line.** In reported cases of atypical and adult PRRT2 epilepsy, carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine are still the foundation of treatment and are effective in the majority of cases. [(Clinical and neuroimaging features of PRRT2-related epilepsy in adult patients, 2024 — PMID 41554218)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41554218/)

**Drug-resistant cases have been documented.** A minority of patients with atypical PRRT2 epilepsy do not achieve seizure control with sodium channel blockers alone. These cases are described in case series but are not yet numerous enough to support formal second-line recommendations. Management defaults to standard refractory epilepsy protocols under specialist direction.

**Longer treatment courses are needed.** Unlike the self-limited infant presentation, persistent and adult-onset PRRT2 epilepsy does not resolve on its own. Treatment is ongoing, and the expectation of a time-limited course — appropriate for BFIS — does not apply.

**Monitoring and EEG findings differ.** A 2024 study of adult PRRT2 epilepsy patients found epileptiform discharges in the majority of cases, unlike the typically normal interictal EEGs in BFIS. This suggests that routine EEG findings may be a useful tool for monitoring in atypical presentations, though this has not been formally studied. [(PMID 41554218)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41554218/)

### A note on treatment decisions without a diagnosis

Many adults with PRRT2-related epilepsy may be receiving treatment for epilepsy without a genetic diagnosis — managed as idiopathic or cryptogenic epilepsy. In these cases, the treatment may or may not include a sodium channel blocker, depending on seizure type and prior workup. A PRRT2 diagnosis does not necessarily change the medication regimen, but it changes the context: it identifies the mechanism, explains the family history, and resolves decades of diagnostic uncertainty.

***

{% hint style="info" %}
**Foundation Note**

The treatment guidance for PRRT2-associated epilepsy was built around the most common and most studied presentation: self-limited infantile seizures that respond well and resolve. For that presentation, the guidance is solid and the outcomes are good.

For everyone else — the adult with persistent seizures, the patient whose epilepsy did not resolve, the person whose PRRT2 was found on an epilepsy panel decades after their first seizure — the formal literature offers much less. Treatment in these cases rests on the same medications that work for the typical case, applied by clinicians extrapolating from incomplete evidence.

This is not a failure of treatment. Sodium channel blockers work across the spectrum. It is a failure of research — specifically, the absence of systematic study of PRRT2 epilepsy in adults. Until that research exists, atypical patients are managed on the strength of a mechanism, not a guideline. The Foundation's position is that they deserve both.
{% endhint %}
