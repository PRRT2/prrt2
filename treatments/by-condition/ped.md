# Treatment for PED

Treatment for paroxysmal exertional dyskinesia depends heavily on which gene is responsible. The underlying mechanisms are different, the logic of treatment is different, and applying the wrong approach is not just ineffective — it may delay access to what actually works. Before committing to a treatment plan, genetic clarification matters.

(See [Paroxysmal Exertional Dyskinesia](../../conditions/ped.md) for the clinical picture and the evidence that both PRRT2 and SLC2A1 cause this phenotype.)

## Established: What the genetics determine

### GLUT1-related PED (SLC2A1)

When PED is caused by SLC2A1 mutation and confirmed GLUT1 deficiency, the **ketogenic diet** is the primary intervention and is often highly effective. Its mechanism here is specific: the diet provides ketone bodies as an alternative brain fuel, bypassing the broken glucose transporter at the blood-brain barrier. This is not a general anti-excitability effect — it addresses the root metabolic cause. The diet requires medical supervision; implementation without specialist oversight is not appropriate.

Sodium-channel medications — carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine — which work well in PRRT2-PKD, are generally not the right tool here. The mechanisms do not align.

### PRRT2-related PED

There is no single established first-line treatment for PRRT2-PED, and the evidence base is less developed than it is for PRRT2-PKD. What is known:

**Sodium-channel medications** used for PKD may offer some benefit in PRRT2-related exertional episodes, but the response is less consistent and less dramatic than in classic kinesigenic PKD. This fits the underlying biology: PKD's tight kinesigenic trigger maps more cleanly onto the rapid-firing dynamics that sodium-channel blockers address. Exertional triggering involves a longer, sustained neuronal load, which may not respond identically. Carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine remain reasonable to discuss with a specialist for PRRT2-PED, with realistic expectations.

**Acetazolamide** is worth specific consideration for exertional and vibration-triggered presentations in PRRT2 carriers. It works through a different mechanism than carbamazepine — carbonic anhydrase inhibition rather than sodium-channel blockade — and has an established evidence base for exercise-induced dyskinesia. For carriers who have not tolerated carbamazepine, or whose exertional pattern is prominent, it is a logical alternative to raise with a neurologist.

{% hint style="info" %}
Carbamazepine caused significant cognitive effects in some PRRT2 carriers — enough to affect daily function and driving. If you have tried it and experienced this, acetazolamide's generally milder cognitive profile makes it a relevant option to discuss with your team. See [Acetazolamide](../medications/other-medications.md) in Other Medications.
{% endhint %}

## Emerging: Ketogenic diet as general channelopathy support

For GLUT1-related PED, the ketogenic diet is established and mechanism-specific. For PRRT2, the question is different but not baseless.

Research into how the ketogenic diet reduces neuronal hyperexcitability shows it acts on multiple targets beyond glucose transport, including direct modulation of ion channels. Polyunsaturated fatty acids elevated by a ketogenic diet modulate sodium and potassium channel activity — the same channel systems that PRRT2 normally regulates. This is a separate pathway from the GLUT1 fuel-bypass effect. In animal models of potassium channelopathy (Kcna1-null mice), the ketogenic diet has demonstrated anti-seizure efficacy, suggesting its anti-excitability effects extend to non-GLUT1 channelopathies.

No clinical trial has tested the ketogenic diet specifically in PRRT2 carriers, and it cannot be recommended as established treatment for PRRT2-PED. What can be said is that the mechanistic rationale for some benefit is not absent — it is grounded in ion-channel biology — and that some carriers who adopt a low-carbohydrate or ketogenic diet for other reasons report subjective improvement in paroxysmal symptoms.

This is an area where the evidence is building, the logic is coherent, and the lived experience of the community warrants attention.

{% hint style="warning" %}
**🧭 Foundation Note**

*The PRRT2 Foundation’s own synthesis and lived experience, offered alongside the established science — not as established medical fact.*

The ketogenic diet question comes up regularly in the PRRT2 community. For GLUT1 carriers, it can be transformative. For PRRT2 carriers, the situation is less clear — but the framing that keto is "only for GLUT1" misses the broader science. The diet affects neuronal excitability through multiple pathways. Whether that translates to meaningful clinical benefit in PRRT2 is a real and open question, not a settled one.

What the community reports, and what is consistent with the mechanism, is that general metabolic stability matters. Fasting, hypoglycaemia, and high-carbohydrate fluctuations may worsen exertional episodes in PRRT2 carriers — not because of a broken glucose transporter, but because metabolic instability adds to an already dysregulated excitability baseline. Stable fuel, controlled carbohydrate, and avoiding the exertion-plus-fasted state combination are practical strategies regardless of whether a full ketogenic protocol is pursued.

Whether to trial the ketogenic diet is a conversation for your neurologist or a specialist with metabolic expertise. What we can say is that the question deserves engagement, not dismissal.
{% endhint %}

## Managing the exertional trigger

Whatever the genetic cause, sustained exertion is the trigger. Practical management includes:

**Pacing and threshold awareness.** Most carriers can identify approximately how much exertion precedes episodes. Working within that threshold, and building in rest before it is crossed, is the most direct management lever available without medication.

**Fuel timing around exertion.** The evidence for GLUT1 is strong — episodes are worse fasted, and pre-exercise carbohydrate loading helps. For PRRT2, this is less studied, but the general principle that metabolic stability reduces episode frequency is consistent with what carriers report and with the underlying biology.

**Avoiding compound triggers.** In PRRT2, triggers can interact. Exertion alone may not cause an episode, but exertion combined with heat, fatigue, or stress may cross the threshold. Understanding and managing trigger combinations is part of practical daily management. (See [Triggers & Prevention](../../living/triggers-and-prevention.md).)

**Vibration as a distinct exertional trigger.** For PRRT2 carriers, sustained vibration transmission — from power tools, vibrating platforms, or similar sources — appears to function as a specific provocateur distinct from general exertion. Given the channelopathy mechanism and the risk of episode kindling from repeated high-intensity triggers, vibrating exercise equipment is best avoided. This is discussed further in [Lifestyle & Diet](../lifestyle-and-diet.md).
