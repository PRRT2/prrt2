# Treatment for BFIS

{% hint style="info" %}
**This page describes published evidence, not medical advice.** Anti-seizure medication in an infant is a decision for a pediatric neurologist or epileptologist who knows your child's full picture. Bring this page to them — please don't act on it alone.
{% endhint %}

Two things are true at the same time about treating *PRRT2*-related infantile seizures, and holding both is the key to understanding the approach.

**The condition resolves on its own.** In most children seizures stop by around age two, and developmental outcome is expected to be normal.

**And most infants still need medication.** Not because the outlook is poor, but because the seizures arrive in clusters — up to eight to ten brief seizures per day, every two to three hours — and that burden is too high to simply wait out. In one cohort of genetically confirmed *PRRT2* infants, over 90% required anti-seizure medication in the first year, and nearly half needed intravenous medication for rapid control.

So treatment isn't a fight against a progressive disease. It's bridging your child safely across a window that will close on its own.

***

## Why sodium channel blockers

The *PRRT2* protein normally acts as a brake on sodium channels in neurons — it's a negative modulator of Nav1.2 and Nav1.6. Lose one working copy of the gene, and that brake weakens; neurons over-fire. Medications that block sodium channels substitute for something the missing protein was doing.

The mechanism and the clinical results point the same direction. **Carbamazepine** and **oxcarbazepine** are the agents most often described as first-line for *PRRT2*-related seizures.

What the cohort data shows:

| Finding | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Sodium channel blocker started first | **Every** infant had their last seizure before age one (4–9 months). Only 2 of 9 needed a second drug — one for rash, one for a low blood level, neither for failed control. |
| Non-sodium-channel-blocker started first | **75%** needed a second medication because seizures weren't controlled. |
| Carbamazepine response rate | **86%** — highest of any single agent in that series |
| Valproic acid response rate | **63%** |
| Levetiracetam / phenobarbital first-line | Largely failed to control the initial clusters; nearly all required an additional medication |
| Levetiracetam → oxcarbazepine switch | In a separate series, 8 infants with clusters uncontrolled on escalating levetiracetam **all** remitted after switching to oxcarbazepine |

Lacosamide and low-dose oxcarbazepine have also been reported to work well, and lamotrigine has been raised as a promising option given its favorable side-effect profile — though the evidence for it in infants is thinner.

***

## The honest caveats

Three things belong alongside that table.

**1. The evidence base is cohort studies, not randomized trials.** GeneReviews states explicitly that carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine "lack extensive study" for this specific indication — the rationale is partly extrapolated from how well they work for *PRRT2*-related PKD. The signal across series is consistent. It is not the same as trial-grade proof.

**2. Other first-line medications do work for some children.** GeneReviews notes that seizures in self-limited infantile epilepsy respond to first-line anti-seizure medications generally, including levetiracetam. Where a therapeutic level is needed fast, phenobarbital is used in newborns and levetiracetam or lacosamide in infants for that reason. Medication should be chosen on the best overall risk-benefit profile for your child, not on one rule.

**3. Carbamazepine carries real risks.** These include serious hypersensitivity reactions. One published *PRRT2* patient developed Stevens-Johnson syndrome on carbamazepine and was subsequently well controlled on oxcarbazepine. This is a conversation about your individual child, not a default setting.

***

## Rescue plans and seizure clusters

Because this condition presents in clusters, **rescue medication matters more here than in many infantile epilepsies.** GeneReviews describes rescue medications — most commonly benzodiazepines such as lorazepam, diazepam, or midazolam — as *crucial* for seizures lasting longer than five minutes and for seizure clusters.

If your child has a cluster-prone seizure disorder and you don't yet have a **written** rescue plan, that is a concrete, specific thing to request at your next appointment. It should cover:

* Which rescue medication, at what dose, by what route
* How long to wait before giving it
* What to do if a cluster starts rather than a single seizure
* When to call emergency services

GeneReviews also lists one preventive measure specifically for this diagnosis: **treat fevers promptly.**

***

## Coming off medication

Seizures typically subside by age two, which means there's usually no need for prolonged medication beyond that point.

Timing the withdrawal is your neurologist's call. Two considerations from the literature: anecdotal reports suggest seizures can recur if medication is withdrawn during the **first** year of life, and withdrawal somewhere between **12 and 18 months** has been proposed in the ILAE literature.

Monitoring during and after treatment depends on the medication used, and may include periodic blood levels, electrolytes, liver enzymes, complete blood counts, and vitamin D. Repeat EEG is advised if subclinical seizures are suspected.

***

## Monitoring development

Heterozygous *PRRT2* variants are **not** known to carry an increased risk for developmental abnormalities. GeneReviews nonetheless recommends monitoring early developmental progress **at every visit**, on the general principle that any seizure disorder in infancy carries a theoretical risk.

If you have developmental concerns about your child, raise them. They deserve evaluation on their own merits rather than dismissal because the diagnosis is labeled favorable.

***

## What isn't a treatment target yet

**No intervention has been shown to reduce the risk** of later developing paroxysmal kinesigenic dyskinesia. Management focuses on controlling seizures and monitoring development — there is nothing preventive you are failing to do.

More broadly: **no medication has ever been developed specifically for *PRRT2*.** Everything above is a medication built for epilepsy generally, used because it happens to fit the mechanism. For infantile seizures that fit works genuinely well. Elsewhere on the spectrum it fits far less well. See [Future Therapeutics](../../research/future-therapeutics.md) for where targeted treatment research stands.
