# Treatment for PKD

Paroxysmal kinesigenic dyskinesia is, for most people, **highly treatable** — and the approach is well established. This page describes how that treatment typically unfolds.

## First line: a low-dose sodium-channel medication

The standard first-line treatment is **[carbamazepine](../medications/carbamazepine.md)** (or its close relative oxcarbazepine). The response in PKD is frequently dramatic, and a striking feature is that the effective dose is often **much lower than the doses used for epilepsy**. Many people go from multiple episodes a day to none.

This strong, low-dose response is so characteristic that clinicians sometimes regard it as supporting the diagnosis itself.

## When first-line treatment isn't the full answer

If carbamazepine is not tolerated, interacts with other necessary medications, or does not fully control episodes, alternatives include:

* **[Lacosamide](../medications/lacosamide.md)** — a newer sodium-channel agent.
* **Other sodium-channel medications** such as oxcarbazepine and phenytoin (see [Other Medications](../medications/other-medications.md)).

Switching or adjusting among these is a specialist's task, guided by your response and tolerability.

## The role of trigger management

Because PKD episodes are provoked by sudden movement and startle, **practical strategies around triggers** complement medication: easing into movement rather than starting abruptly, managing stress and fatigue (which lower the threshold), and similar measures. These are covered in [Lifestyle & Diet](../lifestyle-and-diet.md). For well-controlled patients on medication, triggers matter less; for those between adjustments, they can make a real difference.

## The long view

PKD often **improves with age** — episodes may lessen or even resolve over time in some people, and medication needs can change accordingly. Any change to treatment should be made with your specialist, not on your own.
