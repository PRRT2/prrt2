# Lifestyle & Diet

Because PRRT2 episodes are so often **provoked**, the ordinary details of daily life — how you move, how you sleep, what and when you eat, how much stress you are under — are a genuine part of management, not a footnote to it. None of this replaces medication where medication is needed, but for many people it meaningfully changes how often episodes occur.

## Managing triggers

The most direct lever is reducing the things that set episodes off. Common triggers across PRRT2 conditions include:

* **Sudden movement and startle** (the classic PKD trigger) — easing into movement, rather than launching abruptly from rest, can reduce kinesigenic episodes.
* **Sustained exertion** (the PED trigger) — pacing activity and recognizing your threshold.
* **Stress and strong emotion** — among the most commonly reported triggers, and one reason stress management has real physical payoff.
* **Fatigue and poor sleep** — being run-down lowers the threshold for episodes.
* **Illness, fever, and overheating** — episodes often cluster when the body is under strain.

Identifying *your* triggers is where [Symptom Tracking](../diagnosis/symptom-tracking.md) earns its keep — patterns that are invisible day to day often become clear in a written record.

## Sleep

Sleep deserves its own mention. Insufficient or disrupted sleep is a frequent aggravator of paroxysmal episodes, and sleep is also a domain in which PRRT2's effects on neural excitability plausibly extend. Protecting consistent, sufficient sleep is one of the most reliable lifestyle measures available, and worth treating as part of the medical plan rather than as separate from it.

## Diet

Diet matters in a few specific ways:

* **The ketogenic diet** has an established, central role in **GLUT1-related PED** (SLC2A1), where it can be highly effective. It is a medically supervised diet, not something to undertake casually. (See [Treatment for PED](by-condition/ped.md).)
* **General nutrition** supports overall neurological resilience, as it does in any chronic neurological condition.

## Caffeine, alcohol, and other substances

Responses vary from person to person, but stimulants, alcohol, and disrupted routines can all influence episode frequency for some people. This is an area where personal tracking is more useful than any general rule — watch your own pattern and discuss what you find with your specialist.

{% hint style="warning" %}
**🧭 Foundation Note**

_The PRRT2 Foundation’s own synthesis and lived experience, offered alongside the established science — not as established medical fact._

One lever that is under-discussed in the formal literature, but that comes up repeatedly in lived experience, is **blood-sugar stability**. Some people in our community find that large swings in blood sugar — long gaps without eating, heavily refined-carbohydrate meals, the crash that follows — seem to coincide with worse or more frequent episodes, particularly the tone-loss type.

We are careful here: this is an observation, not an established finding, and it is not a recommendation for any specific diet. But it costs nothing to notice. If you track your episodes alongside your meals and notice a pattern around blood sugar, that is worth raising with your clinician — and steadier, more regular eating is a low-risk thing to try while you do.
{% endhint %}

> Informational only — not dietary or medical advice. Major dietary changes (especially the ketogenic diet) should be made with medical supervision.
