# For Your Doctor

This page is designed to be **shared with a clinician** — printed, or pulled up on a screen during an appointment. Because PRRT2 is rare and under-recognized, patients often find themselves introducing the condition to clinicians who have not encountered it. This is a concise, neutral summary to make that conversation productive.

> **For clinicians:** This is patient-prepared educational material, not a clinical guideline. The authoritative clinical resource is **GeneReviews** (linked below), and management should follow your own judgment and current literature.

## PRRT2 in brief

PRRT2 (Proline-Rich Transmembrane Protein 2; chromosome 16p11.2) is a presynaptic protein involved in regulating neurotransmitter release and neuronal excitability, including interactions with the SNARE complex (SNAP-25) and, per more recent work, voltage-gated sodium channels. Pathogenic variants — most commonly the frameshift **c.649dupC** (~80% of cases) — act through **loss of function / haploinsufficiency**.

Inheritance is **autosomal dominant with incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity**. There is **no established genotype–phenotype correlation**, and the associated conditions form a **continuous, often age-evolving spectrum** within individuals and families.

## The associated conditions (one spectrum)

* **PKD** — brief (typically under a minute), movement-triggered dyskinetic/dystonic episodes; preserved consciousness; normal exam between episodes; onset usually childhood/adolescence.
* **BFIS / self-limited familial infantile epilepsy** — focal infantile seizures, typically first year of life, usually remitting in early childhood.
* **ICCA** — co-occurrence of BFIS and PKD.
* **Hemiplegic migraine** — PRRT2 is among the associated genes.
* **PED** — less common with PRRT2 (consider SLC2A1 / GLUT1).

Note: recent literature describes **atypical and occasionally drug-resistant or non-remitting phenotypes** — PRRT2-related conditions are **not invariably benign**, and adults may carry persistent symptoms.

## Key management points

* **PKD** is frequently and dramatically responsive to **low-dose sodium-channel blockers** — **carbamazepine** or **oxcarbazepine** first-line, often at doses well below those used in epilepsy. **Lacosamide** and other agents are alternatives.
* **Hemiplegic migraine** requires migraine-specific management; certain standard migraine agents are typically avoided — specialist input advised.
* **PED** management depends on the underlying gene; GLUT1-related PED often responds to a **ketogenic diet**.
* **Focal dystonia and spasmodic dysphonia** (where present) may be addressed with **botulinum toxin**.

## Diagnostic notes

* **PRRT2 is included on many dystonia, movement-disorder, and epilepsy gene panels** — it may already be testable through panels you order routinely.
* A strong, low-dose response to sodium-channel blockade in a kinesigenic paroxysmal disorder is itself clinically suggestive.
* Episodes are frequently **misattributed** to epilepsy, functional/psychogenic disorders, tics, or anxiety; preserved consciousness and a kinesigenic trigger help distinguish PKD.
